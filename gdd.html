01

Premise & story

You've just got a brand new campervan — a lifelong dream. Your partner is about to move across Europe to be with you, and together you hatch a plan: drive from your city to hers, pack up her entire life (and her cat), and drive back. All within a week.

What starts as a road trip becomes something more — a test of your relationship, your resourcefulness, and your ability to sleep in a van at a dodgy petrol station at 2 AM while a cat sits on your face.

Inspired by a true story. The game is based on a real journey — a couple who drove a campervan from Barcelona to Vilnius and back, carrying everything that mattered: boxes, a cat, a drone, and the kind of trust that only a thousand-mile detour can build.

Narrative pillars

  • Cozy despite the danger — the van is warm, the road is uncertain. The contrast is the soul of the game.
  • A relationship tested and strengthened — the journey is hard, but you come out the other side closer than before.
  • Real road trip texture — your first stop at a supermarket in a language you don't speak, her choosing every song, chasing the perfect light for one last drone shot. The mundane IS the magic.
02

Game structure

The game is divided into two legs — outbound and return — with distinct challenges. A full run tells a complete story.

Leg 1 — Outbound

  • Start with a brand new, empty van
  • First task: prepare the van for living
  • Drive to your partner's city
  • Van is light, spacious, fresh
  • Learning the systems, building confidence

Leg 2 — Return

  • Van is now packed with boxes + cat
  • Less space = lower comfort = morale pressure
  • Heavier van = more fuel, slower, harder to handle
  • But you know the road better now
  • Arriving home together = the real reward

Core loop

Each day is about survival. Drive as far as you can, manage your resources, find somewhere to sleep, and hope nothing goes wrong in the night. The goal is simple: reach the destination before the trip falls apart.

The roguelike frame

This is a roguelike. Your first van is terrible, your first camera is a phone, your gear is minimal. When the trip fails — van breaks down, money runs out, morale collapses — something strange happens. Your pet looks at you with those knowing eyes, and everything rewinds. You're back at the start, but not quite from scratch.

The guardian rewinder: The pet is more than a companion — it's a mysterious being that refuses to let the story end badly. The couple doesn't know. Only the player shares this secret with the pet. Each run, the pet carries forward what matters: your photos, your reputation, your knowledge of what's out there. On the final successful run, the pet can finally just... be a pet.
03

Van preparation

The game begins before the road does. You have a brand new, empty van. Before you can leave, you need to make it liveable — and you won't find everything in your starting city. Part of the fun is buying gear along the way, in different countries, slowly turning a vehicle into a home.

Essential gear categories

Living essentials

  • Bed sheets & blankets — sleep quality, warmth
  • Kitchen gear — pots, pans, utensils, plates
  • Curtains / blinds — privacy, daytime rest, warmth
  • Storage containers — organisation affects comfort

Maintenance & systems

  • Cleaning supplies — hygiene rating
  • Water hose — for filling the fresh water tank
  • Gas canisters — powers the stove
  • Solar panel / battery — electricity (van-dependent)
  • Basic tools — for roadside repairs
Regional availability: Not everything is available everywhere. Some items can only be found in specific countries or regions — a snow chain shop near the Alps, a cheap camping store in a Baltic town. This encourages exploration and makes each run feel like a shopping adventure across Europe.
04

Phase 1 — Driving

Goal

Cover as much distance as possible toward the destination without wrecking the van or running out of fuel — while keeping everyone alive, fed, and sane.

The pilot

  • Drives the van — avoiding accidents, potholes, weather hazards
  • Manages speed vs. fuel consumption tradeoff
  • Keeps conversations going with the partner (affects morale)
  • Monitors energy, hunger, and bathroom needs

The co-pilot / photographer

  • Manages the map and navigation — spots upcoming stops, plans detours
  • Controls the radio / music — she picks every song, and that's part of the magic
  • Keeps energy and morale up — suggests breaks, activities, conversations
  • Spots gas stations, rest areas, and points of interest
  • Manages resources — tracks food, water, fuel levels
  • Full camera control — drone (aerial/cinematic), GoPro (action), phone (quick shots). Photography is a core objective, and the co-pilot owns all of it.
One drives, one captures. The role split creates natural asymmetry — the pilot focuses on the road, the photographer focuses on the world. Driving alone while your partner sleeps is possible but lonely — morale drops, you miss photo opportunities, and fatigue builds faster. Cooperation isn't a mechanic; it's the whole game.

Exploration stops

  • Gas stations — fuel, snacks, bathroom, basic supplies. Sometimes the only overnight option.
  • Towns — shops, mechanics, gear. Regional items only available locally.
  • Supermarkets — food, cleaning supplies, random useful finds.
  • Points of interest — scenic spots, rare items, photo opportunities.
  • Water fill points — refill the fresh water tank (not always easy to find).

Weather, temperature & climate

Temperature and weather are not cosmetic — they are a core system that affects every part of the game. Driving from Southern Europe to the Baltics means crossing climate zones, and the van reacts to all of it.

  • Freezing temperatures — if the van is parked outside in sub-zero weather, the water system freezes. No sink, no shower, no toilet. The windshield ices over. You need to thaw everything before you can move.
  • Ice roads — GPS might suggest a route, but without winter tires or snow chains, icy roads are impassable. You're forced to find another way or risk sliding off the road.
  • Snow — reduces visibility, slows driving, increases fuel consumption. Heavy snow can block roads entirely.
  • Rain — wet roads increase stopping distance. Heavy rain limits visibility and makes roadside rest spots miserable.
  • Heat — southern stretches in summer overheat the van. Food spoils faster. The cat gets stressed. You need ventilation but open windows attract bugs.
From the real trip: When we arrived in Lithuania, it was snowing. The van was parked outside at a hostel while we packed everything — a multi-day job. All the water in the van froze solid. No bathroom, no shower, no sink. The windshield was a block of ice. Later, the GPS sent us down a road that was pure ice — no winter tires meant we physically couldn't enter. These moments are terrifying in real life and thrilling in a game.
05

Phase 2 — Rest & sleep

Key design note: Rest is the requirement — you physically cannot keep driving forever. Where you choose to sleep determines what might happen during the night. Night is the most natural time to rest, but a peaceful daytime spot with curtains drawn can work too. The tradeoff: resting during the day costs driving hours.

Finding a spot

The perfect spot is safe, has great views, and is peaceful. But sometimes the only option is a shady gas station parking lot. The player decides when to stop and where — and that choice has consequences.

  • Campgrounds — safe, expensive, may require detour. Deep rest, events are rare and mild.
  • Well-lit rest areas — moderate safety, accessible. Decent rest, occasional events.
  • Roadside / forest clearings — free, risky. Light rest, frequent events.
  • Gas station parking — grim, but available. Light rest, unpredictable.
  • Daytime rest — curtains drawn, peaceful spot. Short but safe rest, costs driving time.

The sleep timelapse

When you go to sleep, you choose how many hours to rest. Then you watch the night pass as a timelapse — the sky changing, the van settling, ambient sounds shifting. It's not a skip; it's a visual experience.

But depending on where you parked, events may interrupt the timelapse. A noise outside. An animal scratching at the van. Someone knocking on the window. A sudden storm. The location determines the probability and severity of events — sleep in a campground and you'll likely rest peacefully. Sleep in a forest clearing and you might not sleep at all.

Possible night events

  • Strange noises — wake up, lose sleep quality
  • Someone tries the door handle — panic moment
  • Animal encounter — could damage the van
  • Storm rolls in — van rocks, leaks if damaged
  • Pet gets restless — needs attention mid-sleep
  • Beautiful sunrise — wake early for a great photo

What affects events

  • Location type (campground vs. roadside vs. forest)
  • Region and country
  • Weather conditions
  • How visible/hidden the van is
  • Days elapsed in the run
  • Pure luck

Rest quality → next stretch

Good rest means sharper reflexes, better morale, and sometimes a bonus. Bad rest means sluggish driving, lower morale, and debuffs. No rest at all leads to hallucinations, swerving, and the partner refusing to continue.

06

Cargo & the return

When you arrive at your partner's city, the game shifts. Now you have to pack everything into the van — boxes of belongings, furniture pieces, personal items — and fit them in alongside your living setup. Then drive all the way back.

Packing the van

  • A spatial puzzle: fit boxes into limited van space
  • Some boxes end up on top of the bed — directly impacts comfort and rest quality
  • Heavier load = more fuel consumption, slower driving, harder handling
  • Poorly packed = things shift and break on rough roads

Return leg differences

  • Less space: the van is cramped. Morale pressure increases. Comfort drops.
  • Heavier: fuel consumption goes up. The van handles differently.
  • The cat is here now: adds a whole new resource management layer.
  • But you know the road: routes you explored on the way there are partially revealed. You have gear and experience.
  • Higher stakes: you're carrying someone's entire life in the back.
Design intent: The return should feel fundamentally different from the outbound trip — same systems, but the weight (literal and emotional) changes every decision. Getting home together, with everything intact, is the real victory.

Resources

The player manages a tight web of interconnected resources. The van is a living system — everything connects to everything.

ResourceRole
FuelDrives the van. Consumed faster at high speed, rough terrain, or with heavy cargo.
Fresh waterEssential for cooking, drinking, and hygiene. Refilled at water points (not always easy to find). Tank capacity is limited.
Grey waterDirty water from sink/shower. Needs to be dumped at service points. Full tank = can't use the sink.
Black waterToilet waste. Must be emptied at dedicated facilities. Neglect = hygiene disaster.
GasPowers the stove for cooking. No gas = cold meals only = morale hit.
ElectricityPowers lights, devices, fridge. Solar panels help but aren't always enough. Some vans depend on it more than others.
FoodFeeds you and your partner. Quality matters — good meals boost morale, snacks just keep you alive.
Camera gearPhone (always available), GoPro, drone. Better gear = better photos = more income. Unlocked across runs.
Van healthDamaged by driving hazards and rest encounters. At zero, the run is over.
MoneyBuy everything. Finite — earned through side tasks, scavenging, or content creation.
MoraleBoth partners. Low morale = navigation penalties, arguments, forced stops.
Pet suppliesSpecies-specific food, waste management supplies. Neglect triggers chaos events and escape attempts.
GearSnow chains, spare tire, tools, curtains, hose. Required for specific challenges.

Human needs

Both characters have personal meters that affect gameplay:

  • Energy — depletes while driving, restored by rest. Low energy = dangerous driving, debuffs.
  • Hunger — regular meals needed. Cooking a real meal is better than snacking but takes time and resources.
  • Bathroom — needs periodic stops or use of the van's facilities (which generates black water). Ignoring it tanks morale fast.
  • Hygiene — uses fresh water. Low hygiene = morale drop and potential health events.
08

Companion & morale

Your partner isn't just a passenger — she's a game system and a person. The relationship is the emotional core of the game. Keeping each other happy isn't just strategy — it's the point.

What affects morale

  • Positive: good meals (cooked, not just snacks), scenic stops, safe rest spots, taking breaks, good music choices, interesting conversations, photo opportunities, a happy cat
  • Negative: driving 5+ hours without a break, cold meals, disturbed rest, ignoring the cat, van damage, cramped space (especially on the return), bad music choices, arguments

Morale effects

  • High: better navigation hints, helps scavenging, spots dangers early, better photos, finds great sleep spots
  • Low: refuses to navigate, demands stops, won't help set up camp, triggers arguments that debuff driving, silent treatment (no co-pilot bonuses)
Design intent: Morale should feel organic, not punitive. The game should make you want to pull over at that sunset viewpoint even though you're behind schedule — because the photo will be beautiful, your partner will love it, and that's what this trip is really about.
09

The pet

The pet is the heart of the game — and its deepest secret. To the couple, it's a beloved companion that joins the journey on the return leg. To the player, it's something more: a guardian that exists outside of time, quietly rewinding the story whenever things go wrong.

The rewinder. The couple doesn't know. They think they're on a road trip. But the pet has done this before — many times. When the van breaks down for good, when the money runs out, when everything falls apart, the pet looks at you with those knowing eyes... and time resets. Photos survive. Reputation carries over. The pet remembers everything. On the final successful run, it can finally just be a pet.

Pet selection

Players choose their pet at the start. Each species has different needs, chaos potential, and rewind flavor:

  • Cat — nine lives, literally. Subtle, mysterious. Blinks and time rewinds. Feels like it exists outside of normal rules. Independent but occasionally chaotic.
  • Dog — loyal, dramatic. Howls at the moment of failure, time cracks and resets. More emotionally expressive — whines near previously dangerous spots, gets excited near good ones.
  • Others (unlockable) — a parrot that "repeats" the timeline, a turtle that's "been through this before." Each with unique mechanics and personality.

Needs

  • Regular feeding (species-specific food resource)
  • Waste management (litter, walks, etc.)
  • Doesn't love long drives — stress builds over time
  • Needs attention during rest stops
  • Stress affects behaviour unpredictably

Chaos & charm

  • Neglect → knocks things over, escapes at stops, disrupts sleep
  • Happy pet → subtle warnings, morale boost, adorable
  • Random events: finds items, blocks the gearstick, sits on the map
  • Emotional anchor — the reason you're making this trip
  • Great photo subject (content creation bonus)

Subtle rewinder tells

The pet has déjà vu. It stares at spots where things went wrong in previous runs. It gets nervous before events it's "seen before." It sometimes glances at the camera — at the player — as if sharing a secret. These are never explained in-game. The couple doesn't notice. But the player does.

Escape events

A stressed or neglected pet will try to bolt when a door opens. Escape attempts are dramatic, time-critical events — you have to catch them before they disappear. Panicked reactions can make things worse: slamming a door too fast can cause injury, leading to a vet detour (time + money) and a massive morale hit for both partners.

From the real trip: The cat tried to escape once. In the rush to stop her, the door closed on her paw. She was okay — but the panic, the guilt, the scramble. These are the moments that define a road trip, and they translate directly into high-stakes gameplay events.
10

Content creation

Photography is not a side activity — it's one of the core objectives of the game. One player has full control over all cameras: drone (aerial/cinematic), GoPro (action), and phone (quick shots). Capturing the journey is part of the experience, a source of income, and the primary way the trip is remembered across runs.

Camera role

The co-pilot/photographer owns all camera equipment. This creates natural role asymmetry: the pilot focuses on the road, the photographer focuses on the world. Setting up a drone shot takes time. Catching a fleeting moment with the phone requires quick reflexes. Choosing the right camera for the right moment is a skill.

Photo scoring system

Every photo is scored across four axes:

  • Object rarity — what's in the frame? A common highway guardrail scores low. A wild deer at sunrise scores high. Rare landmarks, weather phenomena, and the pet doing something unexpected are jackpots.
  • Combination synergy — objects that work together score bonus points. Pet + sunset + campsite. Drone + mountains + golden hour. The system rewards composition, not just pointing the camera.
  • Camera angle — drone aerial, low-angle GoPro, eye-level phone — each angle has strengths. The right angle for the right subject multiplies the score.
  • Subject framing — how much of the subject is in frame, how centered, how much negative space. A perfectly framed shot of a distant castle beats a zoomed-in blur.
Design intent: The scoring system should make photography feel like a skill that improves over runs. Early runs with a phone camera produce decent shots. Later runs with a drone and experience produce stunning ones. The pet — who remembers previous runs — subtly positions itself for better shots over time.

Social media feed

  • Content is posted to an in-game social feed (think Instagram)
  • Posts generate income based on quality, timing, and engagement
  • Managing the feed is an optional side mechanic — post too much and followers disengage, too little and you lose momentum
  • Potential follower milestones unlock perks (sponsors, free gear, campground discounts)

End-game gallery

At the end of a successful run, the player is shown a curated gallery of all the content they captured — a visual diary of the journey. This is the emotional payoff: seeing the trip laid out as a story, from the empty van at the start to arriving home together at the end.

Design intent: Content creation should feel like a natural extension of the road trip — not a grind. The best photos come from the best moments, which come from making good decisions. It rewards players who engage with the world instead of min-maxing the route.
11

Roguelike elements

The pet's gift

When a run fails, the pet rewinds time. But it protects certain things across the reset — things it "carries" through the loop:

  • Photos — your gallery persists across runs. The pet protected them.
  • Reputation / followers — the internet remembers, even across timelines.
  • Unlocked van types — better vans become available as you learn.
  • Gear access — you know where to find things now. Shops you discovered stay on the map.
  • Camera upgrades — start with a phone, eventually earn access to drone and GoPro.
First run vs. tenth run: Your first run is a couple in a terrible van with a phone camera and no idea what they're doing. By the tenth run, you have a reliable van, a drone, knowledge of every good campsite between Barcelona and Vilnius, and a pet that seems weirdly calm about everything.

Procedural generation

  • Map layout changes each run
  • Random road events and encounters
  • Shop inventories differ per region and run
  • Some gear only available in specific countries
  • Weather and seasonal conditions shift
  • Night event composition varies by region

Failure states

  • Van health reaches zero — stranded
  • Money runs out — can't refuel or eat
  • Morale collapses — partner refuses to continue
  • Too many sleepless nights — can't drive safely
  • Pet escapes and can't be found — heartbreak ending

Run variety

Different start/end city pairs create vastly different journeys. Barcelona → Vilnius crosses mountain ranges and plains. Lisbon → Helsinki means coastal roads then Scandinavian cold. Each route teaches different strategies and requires different gear — and you buy that gear from the countries you pass through.

The final run

The run where everything clicks. The van holds together, the couple is in sync, the photos are stunning, and you pull into your destination with everything — and everyone — intact. The pet relaxes for the first time. No more knowing glances. No more déjà vu. It's over. You made it home.

12

Multiplayer co-op

The game supports 2-player co-op — both local and online — splitting responsibilities between pilot and co-pilot, just like a real road trip.

Shared camera

Both players share the same camera view — no split screen. You're in the same van, looking at the same road. The co-pilot interacts through UI overlays (map, resource panels, radio) layered on top of the shared view. This keeps the feeling intimate and grounded — you're on this trip together.

Role split

  • Pilot: drives the van, manages speed, avoids hazards, keeps conversations going.
  • Co-pilot: manages the map, navigation, resource tracking, radio/music, spots POIs and gas stations, plans stops.
  • Music: the co-pilot picks the music — finding something that fits the mood of both. (Early idea — mechanics TBD.)
  • Rest phase: both players collaborate on camp setup — choosing where to park, preparing dinner, setting up for the night.
  • Role swap: players can swap pilot/co-pilot roles between stretches.
Co-op philosophy: The co-op mirrors the real dynamic of a road trip. One person drives, the other navigates and manages everything else. You need each other. Communication and planning together is the core fun — and the source of the best arguments.
13

Technical notes

Camera

Single shared camera for both players. No split screen. The co-pilot's UI is overlaid on the same view. This is a core design decision — the shared perspective reinforces the feeling of being on the same journey.

Multiplayer

  • Local co-op (same screen, two controllers or keyboard + controller)
  • Online co-op (same shared camera, streamed to both players)
  • Single-player: AI controls the co-pilot with adjustable autonomy

Character customization

At the start of a new run, players customize:

  • Characters — appearance, sex, name for both the pilot and the companion
  • Van — choose from unlocked van types (starter van at first, more unlocked via meta-progression). Each van has different stats: fuel efficiency, space, durability, built-in systems.
  • Pet — the cat is the default, but other pets could be unlockable (dog, bird, etc.) — each with different needs, chaos potential, and charm.
